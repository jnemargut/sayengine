How the Studio Makes Characters That Don't Look Alike

Technical 🚪 One-way door High stakes

Today every character is the same humanoid template with different settings, which is why the blacksmith and the apprentice look like cousins. You want characters to come mostly from the studio itself, made from a description like any other model, without being stuck with one family of look-alike people. This decides how the studio builds a character, which in turn sets how easily it can be rigged, animated and given a face (decisions 48–49).

Skeleton First, Then Any Body, Face and Outfit

Chunky kid · lanky robot · beaked forest creature · boxy shopkeeper, all made by the studio
1 skeleton: chibi · lanky · stocky · beaked biped · quadruped later2 body from blobs on the bones3 face from feature parts4 hair + outfit parts5 style family + palette
What it feels like: "/studio make a nervous goblin shopkeeper with a long nose and a too-big apron". The studio picks a short, stocky skeleton, shapes a body around it, builds a face from parts (long nose, small eyes, wide mouth), adds an apron, and checks the new character's outline against the rest of your cast so it doesn't look like a cousin.
Build every character the way the studio builds everything else, from ops, starting with its skeleton. You pick or describe a body plan (chibi, lanky, stocky, beaked, and later four-legged), shape the body as smooth blobs around the bones, assemble a face from interchangeable features, and add hair and outfit parts, with a style family and palette on top. Because the studio made the skeleton and face itself, rigging, motion and speech come for free (decisions 48–49). A cast check compares outlines so new characters don't come out as look-alikes. A reference picture can steer proportions and colours.

Works well when

  • Characters made by the studio, from words
  • Very different shapes, not just different settings
  • Everything still walks, talks and holds things

Watch out for

  • The biggest build of the four
  • Blobby bodies need good style rules to look crafted

Keep the one humanoid template and greatly widen its settings: more body types, many face parts, hair and outfit libraries, style families. The smallest change, and everything already built keeps working. But it's still one human underneath, so creatures, chibis and robots are costumes on that body, and the cousin resemblance returns over a big cast.

Works well when

  • Casts of ordinary people
  • Least new work

Watch out for

  • Still one body family
  • No creatures

A Local AI Generator, Then Studio Clean-Up

Picture in → lumpy mesh → studio simplifies and rigs it
TripoSR (MIT, fits 8 GB)remesh to low-polyadd skeleton by landmarks
What it feels like: any picture becomes a character. But on an 8 GB Mac only the smallest generator fits, its output is blobby, and every one needs clean-up and a guessed skeleton before it can move.
Generate a character from a picture or description with a local AI model, then have the studio simplify it to low-poly, add a skeleton and make it move. Any look is possible in principle. But on your 8 GB Mac only TripoSR fits (the better ones, TRELLIS and Hunyuan3D, need 16–24 GB, and Hunyuan's licence excludes the EU and UK), the output is blobby, every machine-learning rigger needs an NVIDIA GPU, and faces would have no speech shapes.

Works well when

  • A bigger machine later
  • Background or statue characters

Watch out for

  • Blobby on 8 GB
  • Rigging and faces are guesses

sculpt with smooth blobsskeleton guessed after
What it feels like: total freedom of shape, like clay. But the skeleton and face come afterwards, by guesswork, so it may not walk or talk well.
Claude sculpts any character shape freely (smooth blended forms, like digital clay), then a skeleton and face are fitted afterwards. The most freedom of shape. But fitting a skeleton and speech shapes to an arbitrary sculpt after the fact is the hard, error-prone direction (it's what the GPU-only research riggers attempt), so motion and lip sync become unreliable.

Works well when

  • Statues and props shaped like creatures

Watch out for

  • Rig and face fitted by guesswork
Respond with: "Option D"

The case against my pick

A is the biggest build here: new skeleton types each need their own walk tuning (the crouched walk showed how fiddly one skeleton already is), and bodies made of blobs can look soft and samey unless the style rules are good. It also replaces the kit that weeks 3–8 built rather than extending it. B gets you real variety in a fraction of the time, and if your games are mostly about people, a much richer human kit may be all you ever notice.

Assumptions and tripwires

  • Assumes today's humanoid kit becomes one skeleton type among several, so the blacksmith and apprentice keep working.
  • Assumes skeletons use the standard humanoid bone names where they apply, so motion and engine export carry over.
  • Starts with bipeds (chibi, lanky, stocky, beaked); four-legged comes later.
  • Tripwire: if three new characters in a row still read as cousins in the cast check, add style families before more skeleton types.
