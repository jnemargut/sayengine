How Pointing at a Moment Works

Technical 🚪 One-way door High stakes

In modelling, "this" means the part you clicked. Once motion, sound or a town joins the view (as your game pulls them in, decision 60), "this" also needs a when: the smith's left foot at 1.4 s, the clink at 0:12, Ada at 10:32 on Saturday, shot 3. Every note you pin, every failed check ("foot slides from 1.2 to 1.5 s") and every proposal will store that address in a file, so once hundreds exist, changing how they're written is painful. That's what makes this the one-way door.

The Thing, Its Own Clock, Whole-Number Ticks

What's stored when you pin "foot slides here"
thing: "Smith/foot.L" clock: "clip:swing" // or game, beats, sound, shot at: 33600 // ticks: 24000 a second = 1.4 s near: "key:strike" // the named moment it sits by len: 7200 // a stretch: 1.4 to 1.7 s of: "swing@v3" // which version you were looking at
Then you say "slower", and the swing gets 20% longer.
The note follows the "strike" moment to its new time and still points at the slide. If the clip changes so much that the moment no longer exists, the note is marked stale and shown to you, never silently moved.
Every address is a thing (a bone, a person, a shot) plus a time measured in that thing's own clock: clip time, game time (with the seed), musical beats, sound time or shot order. Times are stored as whole numbers of ticks (24,000 a second), never as decimals, and can hang off a named moment like "strike" or "grab the handle", so they survive re-timing. A stretch is a start plus a length. This is what Unreal moved to in 2018, after decimal seconds caused drift, and what the film industry's timeline format uses.

Works well when

  • Clips get re-timed ("slower", "hold longer") all the time
  • Music needs beats and the town needs game time
  • Notes should never quietly point at the wrong moment

Watch out for

  • The most fields to get right
  • Ticks aren't human-readable (the view shows seconds)

Option B

The Thing Plus Seconds

What's stored
thing: "Smith/foot.L" t: 1.4 until: 1.7
Then you say "slower", and the swing gets 20% longer.
The note still says 1.4 s, which is now earlier in the swing, before the foot slides. Nothing tells you it moved. Music has the same problem when the tempo changes, and "10:32 on Saturday" can't be written as seconds without the seed and step.
The simplest form: what you pointed at plus a time in seconds, the way Godot stores animation keys. Easy to read and write, and it covers most single clips. But seconds are decimals (1.4 isn't exact in a computer, and long game sessions drift), every kind of time gets squashed into one, and a note can't follow its moment when the clip is re-timed, which our word notes do constantly.

Works well when

  • Clips rarely change once made
  • You want addresses a person can read at a glance

Watch out for

  • Notes point at the wrong moment after re-timing
  • No room for beats or game time

Option C

Frame Numbers at One Project Frame Rate

What's stored
thing: "Smith/foot.L" frame: 42 // at 30 fps to: 51
Then you switch the project to 60 fps for a smoother capture.
Frame 42 now means 0.7 s, not 1.4 s. Every note, check and proposal moves to the wrong place at once. Blender works this way, and changing frame rate there changes how long animations play. Sound and music have no frames at all.
Count frames at one project-wide rate (30 fps), the way Blender and most animators think. Whole numbers, so no decimal drift, and frames match what you scrub through. But the meaning of every address depends on the frame rate staying fixed, sound and music don't come in frames, and re-timing still leaves notes behind.

Works well when

  • Everything is animation at one rate
  • You think in frames

Watch out for

  • Frame-rate changes move every note
  • Doesn't fit sound, music or game time

Option D

A Spot on the Picture, at a Timecode

What's stored
video: "swing-preview.mp4" tc: "00:00:01:12" x: 0.42 y: 0.61 // where you clicked
Then I re-render the preview after a fix.
The note is a dot on an old video. I have to work out which body part was under the dot (it could be the foot or the anvil behind it). The new render has different framing, so the dot no longer lines up. It works for anything visible, including trailers, without any per-system code.
Pin notes to a spot on a rendered video at a timecode, the way Frame.io review works. It needs nothing from each system, so every visible thing gets notes on day one. But the note doesn't know what you meant (I'd guess from pixels), it's lost when the picture is re-rendered, and checks and proposals can't use it, because they need the real thing and time, not a dot.

Works well when

  • Reviewing finished renders like trailers
  • You want notes everywhere with no work

Watch out for

  • I have to guess what the dot meant
  • Notes break on every re-render

The case against my pick

A is the most machinery to build before it pays off: a clock for each kind of time, named moments, version stamps and a stale-note flow. With decision 60's "pulled by your game", maybe only one system joins the view for a while, and plain seconds (B) would serve one clip-based game fine until the second system arrives. If your game is short and its clips rarely change after they're made, B's simplicity wins and the migration later is a script, not a rewrite.

Assumptions and tripwires

  • The view always shows people-friendly times (1.4 s, bar 9, 10:32); ticks live only in the files.
  • Each system adds only its own clock when your game pulls it in (clip first, others as needed).
  • A note whose moment no longer exists is shown to you as stale, never moved silently.
  • Tripwire: if more than about 1 in 10 notes go stale after ordinary edits, anchor to named moments more often.

Research context

  • Unreal switched its Sequencer from decimal seconds to whole-number ticks (24,000 a second) in 4.20. Scripts that mix display frames and ticks are still a common source of errors.Epic: Time Refactor
  • The film industry's timeline format (OpenTimelineIO) stores a moment as a value at a rate, and a stretch as a start plus a length with an exclusive end. A zero-length marker is an instant.OTIO time ranges
  • Even OTIO hit a rate bug: one hour of timecode came out the same at 24 and 23.976 fps.OTIO issue #1452
  • In Blender, changing the frame rate keeps frame numbers and changes how long clips play. Users have asked since 2021 for an option to keep the timing.Blender DevTalk
  • Godot animation tracks are a node path plus a property or bone, with time in decimal seconds. Paths break when nodes are renamed.Godot Animation docs
  • Frame.io doesn't carry comments to new versions. Pasted comments land at the same timecode even if the cut was re-timed.Frame.io Help
  • Figma detaches a comment when its layer is cut and pasted, and there's still no way to reattach it.Figma forum
  • REAPER lets each item follow clock time or musical beats, because tempo changes move them differently.reaper.blog
  • A 32-bit decimal timer is only accurate to 0.24 ms after an hour and 7.8 ms after a day.Bruce Dawson

Where tools disagree

Animation tools (Blender, Godot) favour frames or seconds for simplicity, while editing and engine tools (Unreal, OTIO, USD) learned the hard way to store a rate or clock with every time. Nobody re-anchors notes well after edits. Frame.io and Figma both lose or misplace comments, which is why A anchors to named moments and marks notes stale instead of guessing.
