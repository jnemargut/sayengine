Rigging and Motion for Any Shape the Studio Makes

Technical 🚪 One-way door High stakes

With skeleton-first characters (decision 47), the studio builds every skeleton itself, so rigging (attaching the body to the bones) is solved by construction. The open question is motion: today's walk, run and gestures were tuned for one human body, and the crouched walk showed how easily that goes wrong. A short goblin, a lanky robot and a round beaked creature can't share one recorded walk. This decides how motion adapts to any body plan.

What it feels like: make a new body and it walks right away: stride and step rate come from its leg length (short legs patter, long legs lope), feet plant where they land, and the upright-walk check from week 6 is measured against that body's own leg length. Then a style setting adds personality: "waddle", "swagger", "shuffle".
Walk, run, turn and idle are computed for each body from its own proportions: stride and step rate scale with leg length (the way real animals of different sizes move), feet are placed by leg-reaching (inverse kinematics) so they plant without sliding, and balance follows the body's weight. Style settings (waddle, swagger, shuffle, bounce) add character. Gestures and actions (wave, point, hammer) are written against standard bone names and fitted to each arm length. The week-6 checks become body-relative, so a crouch fails on any body, not just the apprentice's.

Works well when

  • Many different body plans
  • New characters should move immediately
  • Same checks catch the same bugs

Watch out for

  • Computed motion can look generic without style
  • Tuning each body plan once

Keep one set of human motions and retarget them to every body, scaling by hip height (the standard trick for moving clips between skeletons). Instant and reuses everything. But it only works for roughly human proportions: chibis and round creatures slide and look like people in costume, which is the problem you're trying to escape.

Give each body simulated muscles and let it learn to walk and balance (an "active ragdoll"). Any body moves physically plausibly, and characters react to being bumped. But training takes hours per body, it's hard to direct ("swagger" isn't a physics term), and the two game engines would need the same physics to match.

The case against my pick

Computed walks tend to look correct but bland, and character in motion usually comes from an animator's hand (B). Generalising the walk to every body plan is real work, and the crouch bug shows how easy it is to get one body wrong, let alone many. If you end up with three or four favourite body plans, hand-tuned motions might look better than any formula.

Assumptions and tripwires

  • Assumes the week 5–6 walk (heel-to-toe foot paths, hips over the planted leg) generalises by scaling with leg length.
  • Assumes each body plan gets one tuning pass for its style defaults, then varies by proportion.
  • Tripwire: if a body plan still looks generic after its style pass, give it hand-tuned overrides (B) for walk only.
