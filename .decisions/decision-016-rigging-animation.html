Where Animation Comes From

Architecture 🚪 One-way door High stakes

Rigging is mostly solved by decision 14: kit characters come with a skeleton, skin weights and standard humanoid bone names, so Godot and three.js both retarget them automatically. The open question is the motion itself: walks, runs, idles, jumps, attacks. Where do those come from, and how does Claude change them when you say "make the walk bouncier"? Whatever you pick, every clip gets the same automated pose checks: pose the character, measure joint volume loss, stretched edges and limbs passing through the body, and render thumbnails for you to review.

In step 2, Claude picks motions from a library of ready-made clips captured or hand-animated by people. Step 3 retargets them onto the kit skeleton; because the bone names are standard, this needs no manual mapping. In the game, Godot's IK (or a three.js equivalent) plants feet on slopes and points heads at things (step 5). The motion quality is human-made and reliable. The limit is that "bouncier" means picking a different clip or tweaking speed, not reshaping the motion.

Works well when

  • You want believable motion on day one
  • Standard actions: walk, run, jump, attack

Watch out for

  • Motion isn't parametric; edits mean swapping clips
  • Library licences vary; check each one

5Complex actions (attacks, emotes) still need clips
# what you'd type /studio make his walk heavier, shorter steps, less arm swing
Motion becomes part of the op graph, like the stool's legs. A walk is generated from settings (stride, bounce, lean, arm swing), so "make the walk bouncier" is one reviewable change in step 3, with before/after in the review panel. It's the most AI-first answer and fits the studio's philosophy exactly. But procedural motion only covers cyclic, physics-like motion well (walks, runs, idles, breathing); one-off actions like a sword swing still need clips (step 5), and good procedural walks take real tuning work.

Works well when

  • Locomotion styles vary per character
  • You want motion edits reviewable like geometry

Watch out for

  • Can look robotic until well tuned
  • Doesn't cover one-off actions

# what you'd type /studio make her do a nervous little victory dance
Describe a motion and have a model generate it. This is the most expressive option and the most magical when it works. But the open models need around 24 GB of GPU memory (step 3), so on your Mac it means a paid cloud service. Output uses the model's own skeleton and needs retargeting, foot sliding is common, and licences for open models may exclude the EU and UK. It's improving fast; worth adding later as an extra source of clips.

Works well when

  • Unusual, expressive one-off motions
  • You have a big GPU or a cloud budget

Watch out for

  • Won't run on your laptop
  • Licence and quality are unsettled

Classic animation: set poses at key moments and let the studio fill in between, with a timeline in the view. It gives full control and works for any motion. But good timing, weight and overlap are a craft, and an AI judging them from still frames does poorly (step 4); the animation benchmarks show this is where agents struggle most. It also means a timeline editor to build. It's best saved as the hand-tweak tool you use on top of A or B.

Works well when

  • You want to animate by hand sometimes
  • Short, specific gestures

Watch out for

  • Weakest AI quality of the four
  • A whole timeline UI to build

The case against my pick

This is close to a toss-up between A and B. A gets good motion fastest, but it's the one place in the whole studio where Claude can't make a precise, reviewable edit. "Bouncier" means swapping clips, which breaks the promise that every change is one setting you can keep or undo. B keeps that promise for locomotion, which is most of what players see most of the time. The tiebreaker: if you care most about shipping a playable character soon, take A. If you care most about the studio's "everything is a setting" idea extending to motion, take B, and add library clips for attacks and emotes.

This recommendation assumes

  • Kit templates (decision 14) export in T-pose with standard humanoid bone names, at most 4 skin weights per vertex, and transforms applied, which avoids most retargeting and import problems in Godot.
  • A free (CC0) or affordable clip library exists for the standard actions; each library's licence is checked before use.
  • B is the planned second step: parametric walk/run/idle replaces library locomotion once the first game is playable.
  • Every clip, whatever its source, passes automated pose checks before export.

What the research says

  • Godot 4.4–4.6 added retargeting, spring bones and IK solvers (two-bone, FABRIK, CCD), and they run after clip playback, so feet plant and heads turn on top of library clips. Godot blog
  • Godot's retargeter relies on common English bone names, and A-pose models need "Fix Silhouette". Templates should ship in T-pose with standard names. Godot docs
  • Open text-to-motion (HY-Motion 1.0) needs 24–26 GB of GPU memory even for its Lite model, outputs on its own 22-joint skeleton, and its licence isn't confirmed for the EU/UK. HY-Motion
  • Mixamo has no official API, and automation goes through unofficial wrappers. Cascadeur's free tier is reportedly non-commercial. Adobe Cascadeur
  • three.js can compute posed vertex positions on the CPU, and glTF requires joints to match their bind matrices. That's enough for a headless pose-check suite measuring joint volume, edge stretch and weight sums, plus the Khronos validator. three.js Khronos
  • Standard skinning loses volume and "candy-wraps" at twisting joints. Twist bones in the template are the game-industry fix. Autodesk
  • AI agents score lowest on animation tasks: 33% on 2D graphics and animation in a Godot benchmark. Judging motion from still frames is a known weakness. GameDevBench
