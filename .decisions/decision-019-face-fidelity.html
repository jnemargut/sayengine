How Detailed Faces Get in Cutscenes

Visual 🚪 One-way door High stakes

You want faces that show emotion and mouths that move with the words, mainly in cutscenes, where the camera comes close. That pulls against decision 12's low-poly style. This decision sets how far faces may break from it. The research is unusually clear on one point: faces look creepy not because they're realistic, but because parts of a character sit at different levels of realism. Each card shows the same blacksmith saying "Hold still" at that level of detail.

Faces stay as simple as the bodies: dot eyes, a slit mouth that opens and closes, and a brow line. Consistent, cheap, and lots of loved games do it. But it can't deliver what you asked for: a mouth that moves realistically with words. One team that started with simplified faces found it "hard to tell what their expressions were" and had to add detail. Cutscene close-ups expose how little a face can say.

Works well when

  • Cutscenes are wide shots or text-driven
  • Minimal style is the point

Watch out for

  • Doesn't meet "mouth moves with words"
  • Emotions are hard to read up close

Stylized, Big-Shape Faces in the Kit

Illustration. Same faceted planes as the body; big eyes, strong brows, a shaped mouth.
~15 mouth + ~15 faceshapes generated per kit
Denser face onlybody stays low-poly
Strongreadable at close-up
Faces keep the kit's faceted, low-poly look, but are designed to act: bigger eyes, strong brow planes, a mouth with enough edge loops to form the 15 standard speech shapes, and a jaw that drops. Only the face region gets denser; the body stays the same. Every expression and mouth shape is generated from the kit template, so each new character gets them for free, and "smile a bit" stays a setting. One level of stylization across the whole character, which is what the research says avoids the uncanny effect.

Works well when

  • Close-up cutscenes with dialogue
  • Many characters from one kit
  • Lip sync tools (6–15 shapes) fit exactly

Watch out for

  • The face template is careful, up-front design
  • Not "realistic"; it's expressive and stylized

Detailed Cutscene Head, Swapped In

Illustration. Two heads per character; the body never changes.
52 shapesfull ARKit set

Mismatch riskhead vs body
Each character gets a second, detailed head with the full 52-shape ARKit face set that swaps in for cutscene close-ups. Mouths can be close to realistic. But it's exactly the mix the research warns about: a detailed face on a low-poly body is a realism mismatch, which is what makes characters feel eerie. It also doubles head work for every character and makes the gameplay and cutscene versions of the same person look different.

Works well when

  • Only a few hero characters ever speak
  • Shots are tight on the face, never the body

Watch out for

  • Textbook uncanny mismatch with the body
  • Two heads to keep in sync per character

Realistic Faces (and Bodies)

Go realistic everywhere: realistic faces need realistic bodies, which means reversing decision 12 (low-poly) and taking on sculpting, detailed textures and the full classic pipeline. Lip sync tools look worst at this level, and players judge against AAA games: Mass Effect Andromeda's faces dominated its reception. Most indies who try it end up polishing only a few hero shots.

Works well when

  • Realism is the game's whole identity
  • You have a big budget for faces

Watch out for

  • Undoes the art style and pipeline you chose
  • Judged against AAA, and harshly

The case against my pick

"Stylized" will never be "realistic", and you did say the mouth should move realistically with words. A stylized mouth with 15 well-blended shapes reads as natural speech, but it won't show the micro-detail of real lips, and a face template that acts well is genuinely hard design: simple faces expose small errors more, not less. If true realism for a few hero characters matters more than consistency, C gets closer, accepting the mismatch risk and double head work.

This recommendation assumes

  • "Realistic mouth" means natural-looking speech (right shapes, right timing, smooth blending), not photoreal lips.
  • Low-poly style (decision 12) stays for the game; faces join it rather than leave it.
  • The kit's face template can carry about 15 speech shapes plus about 15 expression shapes, generated per character, inside a reasonable file size budget.
  • Cutscene cameras can hold close-ups on a stylized face without it falling apart; we'll verify with renders in the first milestone.

What the research says

  • Eeriness comes from parts of one character sitting at different levels of realism. Mismatched eye size or texture was especially eerie, and faces with normal proportions but little detail were rated eeriest. MacDorman et al. 2009 Chattopadhyay & MacDorman
  • Every working lip-sync chain treats the mouth as a small set of shapes: Rhubarb uses 6–9, Meta's standard 15. They work best on stylized faces and look worst on realistic ones. Rhubarb Meta viseme reference
  • The OPUS: Echo of Starsong team started with simplified faces, found it "hard to tell what their expressions were", and added detail. Ghost of a Tale skipped mouth rigging entirely. Game Developer Game Developer
  • Mass Effect Andromeda's facial animation, probably automated at scale with little polish, dominated its reception. PC Gamer Vice
  • In a minimal, symmetrical style, people "notice right away if something is off". Simple faces expose small errors more. Gnog art lead
  • Per-head ARKit services such as Polywink, last published at about $299 per model, and hand tools such as Faceit don't scale to parametric kits that generate many heads. The shapes need generating from the template. CG Channel Faceit

Where experts disagree

AAA facial teams say realism is reachable with enough tech (JALI drives Cyberpunk 2077's lip sync in 10 languages). Indie developers and the uncanny-valley research say stylize and stay consistent, because realism costs more than its payoff. For a solo developer with an AI doing the animation, the indie side has the stronger case.
