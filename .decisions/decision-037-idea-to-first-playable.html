The Road From Loose Idea to First Version

Interaction 🚪 One-way door High stakes

This is the heart of /x-decide-gaming: which decisions come first, and when you first get something to play. Designers agree on a few things (find the fun before polishing, decide camera, controls and character together, art last) and disagree on others (plan versus discover, polished slice versus ugly prototype). Your Terrarium time went into redoing looks and fixing feel, which is exactly what order can prevent.

3Camera, controls, character together + feel passplay it
4The loop around the toy + a 5-minute playtestplay it

6Look, world, cast (a cheap mood pass came at step 2)see it
7First version: one small town, 10 minutes of funplay it
First thing to play: about an hour after the idea
What it feels like: /x-decide-gaming a cozy dawn bread delivery game. Three quick decisions, then a grey cart on grey hills you can push around. Is pushing it fun? Only once it is do the camera, controls and feel get decided, on that toy. Art comes after the fun is proven.
Decide just enough to build a toy, play it, then decide the next thing on top of what you felt. The order follows what designers agree on: the core toy or loop first (Nintendo, Schell, "30 seconds of fun"), camera, controls and character together, a feel pass early (game feel is a design layer, not decoration), scope on one page once the fun is found, and the look last, with a cheap mood pass early so playtests have the right vibe. Every stage ends with something to play, not a document.

Works well when

  • You don't know yet if the idea is fun
  • Feel matters (it did in Terrarium)
  • You want to play within the first session

Watch out for

  • Grey boxes can feel uninspiring
  • Story-led games fit less neatly

Decide everything first (concept, then design), write a one-page design, then build the first version in one go. The closest to decision-kit's product flow and easy to follow. But nobody knows if the toy is fun until the end, and designers from Blow to Cerny say pre-production has to follow what the prototype reveals, not a plan.

Works well when

  • Remakes or genres you know well

Watch out for

  • Fun discovered last
  • Decisions go stale before they're tested

3Play the polished sliceplay it
First thing to play: late, but looks finished
What it feels like: one gorgeous street early, great for a trailer. But if the delivery loop isn't fun, the polish was spent on the wrong thing.
Choose everything needed for one small piece of the game and build it to final quality: look, world, characters, feel. Impressive early, and great for a trailer. But Ron Gilbert calls the vertical slice "one of the dumbest things the game industry has ever come up with" because it spends polish before the design settles, and Riot warns pretty art can hide a game that isn't fun.

Works well when

  • The fun is already proven
  • You need something to show

Watch out for

  • Polish before fun
  • Terrarium's look churn, again

What it feels like: inspiring from minute one, with a mood board and a hero render of the bakery at dawn. But the look gets locked before anyone knows what the game needs.
Start with the pitch and the mood (fantasy, mood board, key art made with the studio), then grey-box the loop. The most motivating start, and it uses the studio's strengths early. But it locks the look before the game is known, the opposite of Nintendo's "gameplay first, then art that works with it", and looks were where Terrarium lost the most time.

Works well when

  • Mood-led or story-led games
  • Pitching to others

Watch out for

  • Look locked too early
  • Play comes later

The case against my pick

Grey boxes are honest but can be deflating, and Riot found prototypes with the right vibe get better playtest feedback; A's cheap mood pass at step 2 has to actually be good, or you'll judge a cozy game on a joyless toy. A toy-first road also suits mechanic-led games better than story- or exploration-led ones (Cook's "arcs"), and a town toy like Terrarium is closer to a toy than a loop, so step 4's "loop around the toy" may be the wrong question for some of your ideas. If your next idea is mood-first, D is the better road.

Assumptions and tripwires

  • Assumes /x-game-build can make a grey-box toy from 3–4 decisions in about an hour with the studio and /game.
  • Assumes the concept skill asks "toy, loop or story?" at step 1 and adjusts the road (story-led ideas get D's order).
  • Tripwire: if the first toy takes more than a session to build, cut step 2 down to one verb and one camera.
  • Tripwire: if you skip playing at steps 2–4 more than once, the road is too slow; merge steps 3 and 4.

Research context

  • Nintendo prototyped Breath of the Wild's physics as a 2D, NES-style mock-up before any content; Mario Odyssey went "gameplay first, then art styles or level design that works well with that gameplay".EngadgetTIME
  • Schell's Lens of the Toy: is it fun to play with, even with no goal? Build the toy first.Schell
  • "30 seconds of fun over and over… if you don't nail that, you're not gonna have a great game."Griesemer
  • Studios lock camera, controls and character together, first.Ubisoft
  • Game feel is a design layer, and about 20 small tweaks transform how a game feels.Juice It or Lose It
  • The vertical slice spends polish before the design settles.Ron Gilbert
  • Riot aims for "ugly but fun" prototypes, but notes the right vibe improves playtest feedback.Riot R&D
  • One-page designs communicate faster and get redrawn instead of rotting.Stone Librande
  • Jam games die from overscoping; winners polish one mechanic.Ludum Dare devlog
  • Loops (skills learned by repetition) and arcs (content used once) are different tools.Lost Garden

Where experts disagree

Mark Cerny wants pre-production to end with a "publishable first playable" at shippable quality; Gilbert and Riot say polish that early wastes work. A reconciles them: find the fun ugly first, then make step 7 the polished first version. Nintendo puts art last; Riot says a cheap mood early helps feedback, which is why A has a mood pass at step 2.
