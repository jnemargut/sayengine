Target Art Style

Visual 🚪 One-way door High stakes

The look your games aim for decides how much of the classic 3D pipeline you need. A flat-coloured low-poly look needs almost nothing beyond what the studio already does. A realistic look needs sculpting, retopology, UV unwrapping, texture baking and painting, which are the exact steps where AI output still breaks down. You asked whether to take on sculpting and UVs; this decision mostly answers that. Each card shows the same small scene in that style, then the pipeline it requires.

Low-Poly, Palette + Material Presets

Solo hits: Megabonk (1M copies in 2 weeks), A Short Hike, DeadPoly. ~$10–100 per prop outsourced.
Shapes are clean and faceted. Every part takes a colour from one shared palette plus a material preset from the materials work now being built (walnut, glazed ceramic, brushed metal), and its gloss, roughness and metalness carry into the engine through glTF. There's no sculpting, no hand-made UVs, no baking and no texture painting. A hundred AI-made assets still look like one game. The limit: shader-drawn detail like wood grain shows in the studio but not in the game; that's what Option E adds later.

Works well when

  • You want to ship games solo, soon
  • Consistency across many AI-made assets matters
  • Runs well on modest hardware

Watch out for

  • Crowded look on Steam; can read as "asset-flip"
  • Less expressive faces and cloth for characters

The case against my pick

Low-poly is the safe, popular choice, and that's its weakness. Steam is full of low-poly games, many built from the same asset packs, and players read the look as cheap unless the art direction is strong. Starting with A also means the wood grain and glaze the studio is learning to render won't appear in your games until the bake step (E) exists, so for a while the game will look plainer than the studio. If you'd rather the game match the studio from day one, pick E now and accept that export work comes first. And characters (decision 14) are where low-poly struggles most.

This recommendation assumes

  • You want to ship games solo or with a tiny team, with Claude doing most asset work.
  • A shared palette plus one shading model is enough to give your games a recognizable look, and a toon/outline shader can add character later.
  • The materials work in progress (presets with colour, roughness, metalness, gloss; procedural wood grain) carries partly into games: preset values travel in glTF; shader-drawn grain does not until a bake step exists (Option E).
  • A is chosen so E is a pure upgrade: same shapes, same presets, plus an export step.

What the research says

  • AI 3D generators are strongest at stylized and low-poly work. Meshy has a dedicated Low Poly mode, and Tripo is the usual recommendation for stylized assets. Prompts drift toward realism unless the style is named. StraySpark Meshy docs
  • Turning any generated object into a game asset still takes retopology, UV layout, material authoring and rigging. Stylized PBR and realistic looks need all of it, plus a high-poly sculpt to bake from. arXiv 2604.23629
  • The standard low-poly method is one material plus a palette or gradient texture, with faces mapped to colour cells. There's no painting, baking or hand UV work, and it maps directly onto a part-by-part model like this studio's. 80.lv
  • Toon shading in Godot is a small shader: stepped light, rim light and outlines, with free versions available. A flat-colour toon look needs no painted textures. godotshaders
  • Low-poly has recent solo hits: Megabonk, a solo 3D low-poly game, sold over 1M copies within two weeks of its 2025 launch. No solo photoreal hits turned up. Outlook Respawn GameDiscoverCo
  • Keeping one style across many AI-made assets is a known, unsolved problem; vendors offer "match these reference images" features, with no independent benchmarks. A shared palette and shader keeps assets consistent by construction. Sloyd Hunyuan3D Studio
  • Outsourced prop costs run about $10–100 for simple low-poly and rise sharply for PBR. The extra cost comes from the texture maps, sculpts and LODs rather than polygon count. RocketBrush Kaedim

Where experts disagree

Indie-market analysts say a solo developer needs a style that doesn't compete with big studios and survives repetition, which means low-poly or toon. Art directors counter that "low-poly" alone is no longer a style, and that standing out takes a strong palette, lighting and silhouette direction on top. Both point the same way: A, plus a deliberate palette and shader, not default flat colours.
