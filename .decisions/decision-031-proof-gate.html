Three tiers, one report. On every change, the Mac renders fixed views with the clock and randomness pinned, in Godot and the web, and compares them with the last approved pictures using a diff made for rendered images. Before a release, it drives real Safari on your iPhone over USB (Apple's own safaridriver, free, no signing). The game reports its frame times and memory, plays for 10 minutes to catch throttling, and is sent to the background and back to catch the lost-picture bug. Release checks read every view's text with the Mac's built-in OCR, look for logos with a small local model, flag wording that claims accuracy, and build the credits list.

  • You want one report, not five scripts
  • Local and free

The case against my pick

Driving a real phone from a laptop is the fiddliest part of the whole suite: it needs the phone plugged in, unlocked and trusted, Safari settings switched on, and Apple can change any of it in an update. Screenshot tests are famously flaky; even three.js's own were "often broken by unrelated PRs", so the Mac tier may cry wolf until tolerances are tuned per scene. One phone is one data point; your iPhone passing says little about an older one. If you only release a few times a year, C's checklist is cheaper than building and maintaining the automation.

Assumptions and tripwires

  • Assumes Safari's remote automation keeps working over USB on current iOS (it has since iOS 13).
  • Assumes your phone's memory limit is measured once (a ramp test) and budgets are set well under it, since Apple publishes no number.
  • Tripwire: if the picture diff flags more than one false change per run for a week, loosen tolerances per scene or switch that scene to budget-only checks.
  • Tripwire: if safaridriver on the phone breaks after an iOS update, fall back to C's checklist for that release.

Research context

  • Apple's safaridriver drives real Safari on a real iPhone over USB (iOS 13+), free and without signing; Appium wraps it.InfoQAppium safari driver
  • The iOS Simulator doesn't enforce phone memory limits: a WebGL page grew to several GB there but was killed quickly on a real phone.WebKit bug 253172
  • Playwright's WebKit is built from WebKit's main branch, not the Safari on your phone, and doesn't reproduce memory pressure.BrowserStack guide
  • Godot's iOS Simulator export is broken and supports only the Compatibility renderer, so Godot iOS testing needs the real phone.Godot proposal
  • three.js pins randomness for its screenshot tests and still finds them "often broken by unrelated PRs".three.js issue
  • Godot can render frame by frame at a fixed rate (--fixed-fps + --write-movie) for repeatable pictures.Godot MovieWriter
  • NVIDIA's FLIP compares rendered images the way people see them and shows where they differ.flip-evaluator
  • No official iPhone Safari memory limit exists; reports range from 512 MB to 800 MB pages, so measure your own phone.WebKit bug 290623
  • Safari loses WebGL after backgrounding on some iOS versions; games must handle context loss.WebKit bug 286297
  • iPhones throttle under long GPU load (about 52% stability over 20 minutes), so test after sustained play.HotHardware
  • The Mac's built-in OCR is free from the command line, and reuse lint checks every file has licence and credit information.mac-ocrREUSE
