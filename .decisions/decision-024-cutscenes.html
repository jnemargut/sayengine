Where Cutscenes Are Made and Played

Architecture 🚪 One-way door High stakes

A cutscene is more than character motion: it's shots, camera moves and cuts, audio, dialogue lines with their performances (decision 23), and events like "door opens". A key fact from the research: glTF, the format that carries your assets, can't hold any of that. It has no audio, no events and no camera cuts. So cutscenes need a home of their own. Where they're made decides whether Claude can direct them ("hold on her face, then cut to his hands"), whether you review them like any change, and whether one cutscene plays in both Godot and three.js.

Cutscenes are a studio file: shots, cameras, cuts, dialogue lines with their performances, audio and events, all plain text. Claude directs it in words; you review it with storyboard frames rendered from the real scene (the strip above) and keep or undo shots like any change. # glTF 2.0 animations can only move nodes and face shapes # KHR_animation_pointer adds more, but not audio or cuts
Bake the whole cutscene into one glTF file: every character, the camera moves and the face shapes as animation. It's the simplest pipeline and uses the asset format you already have. But glTF animation can only move objects and face shapes. It can't play audio, cut between cameras or fire events, and the extension that adds more animatable properties isn't supported by three.js. You'd have to bolt everything else on anyway.

Works well when

  • Silent, single-camera animations
  • Previews and turntables

Watch out for

  • No audio, cuts or events
  • Not a real cutscene format

Render each cutscene to a video file and play it in the game. It looks identical everywhere, and playback is trivial in any engine. But the video can't reflect anything the player changed (outfits, who's present, time of day), files are large, there's a visible switch between real-time and video, and re-rendering is needed for every small change. Reviewing a video is also harder than reviewing a shot list.

Works well when

  • Fixed story moments, high polish
  • Trailers

Watch out for

  • Can't adapt to the player's game
  • Big files; re-render every change

The case against my pick

A means inventing a cutscene format and maintaining two players for it. That's real code, and every time you want a new kind of shot (a camera shake, particles, a slow-motion beat), it has to be added to the format and to both players. Engines already have mature cutscene tools; Godot's AnimationPlayer does nearly all of this natively. If nearly all your cutscenes will be in standalone Godot games, B with Claude editing AnimationPlayer directly is less to build. Keep the format small and give it an "engine-specific" escape hatch, or it grows into an engine of its own.

This recommendation assumes

  • Cutscenes appear in both Godot games and three.js web projects, so authoring once matters.
  • The format stays small: shots, cameras, cuts, lines, audio, events, plus an escape hatch for engine-specific extras.
  • Storyboard frames rendered from the real scene are how you review shots: keep or undo each shot.
  • Branching dialogue lives in a dialogue tool (Yarn Spinner's MIT core, or plain JSON), which triggers cutscenes by name.

What the research says

  • glTF 2.0 animation can only target object position, rotation, scale and face-shape weights: no events, audio or camera switching. KHR_animation_pointer is ratified, and Godot imports it from 4.4, but three.js core doesn't support it. Khronos registry Godot 4.4 needle-tools plugin
  • Godot's AnimationPlayer already holds a whole cutscene: property, transform, face-shape, method-call (event), audio and nested-animation tracks, built for coordinating characters in a cutscene. It's a natural target to generate. Godot docs
  • Firewatch, Oxenfree and Night in the Woods kept dialogue branching in a dialogue tool, separate from animation timelines. Night in the Woods' Yarn scripts even issued the stage directions. GDC: Firewatch dialogue Yarn Spinner
  • Valve's choreography scenes were plain-text files that guides edited by hand, showing that a text cutscene format is workable and diffable. Valve Developer Wiki
  • Yarn Spinner's core and Unity versions are MIT. The Godot GDScript version is alpha under its own licence and needs Godot 4.6+, and the C# port is beta. Yarn Spinner licence
