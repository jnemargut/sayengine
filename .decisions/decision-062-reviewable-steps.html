What Counts as a Reviewable Step Outside Modelling

Technical 🚪 One-way door High stakes

In modelling, each change I make is one step on a branching history. A proposal is a set of steps you keep or undo one by one, and nothing is ever lost. Clips, looks, effects, sounds, music, trailer cuts and town schedules don't work that way yet: I just write a new version. This decides what a "step" is for those things, how big it is, and how far "show me three" goes. It's a one-way door because the history format and every saved proposal depend on it.

What it feels like: exactly like reviewing a model change, for any asset. Each step says in plain words what changed and shows a before and after you have to play. Ask "show me three" and you get three clearly different takes side by side, pick one per shot or per bar, and the rest are thrown away. Only the recipe (the small text file) is kept; the videos and sounds are re-made from it when needed.
Every non-model asset joins the same branching history as models. A step is about one word note's worth of change ("slower", "warmer", "swap the clink"), not every key and not a whole new version. Steps store the recipe, not the rendered output, which is re-made on demand, so the repo stays small. Proposals work exactly as they do for models. "Show me three" works on any asset: three takes, made to be clearly different, with your pick logged. They're compared per section (a shot, a bar), and the losers are discarded, never merged.

Works well when

  • You want the modelling feel everywhere
  • You review in small bites, between other work
  • The public repo should stay lean

Watch out for

  • Each system needs its changes written as named steps
  • Re-making a render takes a few seconds

What it feels like: Frame.io's version stacks. Each time I change an asset, it gets a new version. You compare two side by side and choose. Simple and familiar, but if you like the slower swing and hate the new clink, you can't keep one without the other.
Each asset gets numbered versions, and review means comparing two of them side by side and choosing one, the way video review tools work. It's the least to build and easy to understand. But it's all-or-nothing per version (Figma users have asked for years to merge only parts of a branch), and several changes ride together, so a good change can't be kept without a bad one.

Works well when

  • Changes come one at a time
  • You want the simplest thing

Watch out for

  • Can't keep part of a version
  • Different from how models work

What it feels like: the finest control possible, and the quickest way to stop reading. A single "more weight" note becomes dozens of key changes, and reviewers in every field start clicking "keep" without looking once the list gets long.
Every individual edit (one key, one colour value, one cut point) is its own step, the way Unreal's multi-user editing syncs every change. Nothing could be finer-grained. But one word note turns into dozens of steps, the list stops being readable, and the research is blunt: people review well in small batches and rubber-stamp long lists.

Works well when

  • You want to tune single values by hand

Watch out for

  • Review fatigue, then rubber-stamping
  • Steps don't match how you think ("slower")

What it feels like: image-generator style. Every request returns three takes, you pick, and the next round grows from your pick. Lovely for looks and sounds, but there's no step history to undo, and studies find this style narrows ideas round after round.
Make "show me three" the only way of working: every request returns three takes, you pick one, and the next round starts from your pick, like Midjourney or Photoshop's generative fill. Great for taste-driven things (looks, sounds, music). But it triples the work for simple fixes like "foot slides here", there's no keep-or-undo history, and research finds pick-and-iterate loops converge and lose variety.

Works well when

  • Most choices are about taste
  • You'd rather react than describe

Watch out for

  • 3× the work for plain fixes
  • No undo history; variety narrows

The case against my pick

A asks every system to describe its changes as named, recipe-level steps, which is real work per system: motion must say "swing 20% longer", not just write a new clip. And re-making renders on demand means waiting a few seconds when you open an old step. B is far less to build, and for one game with few changes in flight, version stacks might be all you ever need. The keep-part-of-it problem only bites when several changes land together.

Assumptions and tripwires

  • A step's before and after must be seen (played or viewed) before "keep" counts, and the studio notes when steps are kept without being opened.
  • "Show me three" makes the three clearly different on purpose, because AI alternatives and pick loops tend to converge.
  • Renders are caches tied to the recipe that made them, re-made when the recipe changes, not stored in history.
  • Tripwire: if you keep more than about 9 in 10 steps without opening them, steps are too small or too many. Group them.

Research context

  • Figma's merge is all-or-nothing per branch, and users have asked for years to merge only selected changes.Figma forum
  • Frame.io review works on whole versions: drop v2 on v1, compare side by side, with audio switchable between them.Frame.io comparison viewer
  • Ableton's comping keeps every take on its own lane and picks the best per section. Nothing is destroyed.Ableton Live 12 manual
  • Houdini treats outputs as caches tied to their inputs, marked stale when an input changes. Keep the recipe, re-make the output.SideFX PDG docs
  • Procedural isn't automatically reviewable: Substance graphs are opaque containers with no diff tool, so the recipe has to be plain text you own.Adobe Community
  • Google's median code change is about 24 lines, and review quality falls off on big batches. People review small steps well.Google Research
  • When an AI suggestion was wrong, radiologists' accuracy fell from about 80% to 20–45%, even for veterans. That's why a step's before and after must be seen.Dratsch et al., Radiology 2023
  • Making several options in parallel beat making them one after another: better results, more variety, less defensiveness about critique.Dow et al., Stanford
  • Seeing AI examples narrowed people's ideas, and pick-based feedback cuts variety within a batch. Make the three deliberately different.CHI 2024

Where experts disagree

On how many options to show: the famous jam study found 6 choices sold far more than 24, but later meta-analyses found the effect near zero on average, real only for complex, hard or rushed choices. Three is the industry default (Photoshop, Midjourney) and sits safely below where overload shows up.
