Face, Hands and Body as One Performance

Architecture 🚪 One-way door High stakes

You said the body should look tied to the hands and face, naturally. In real people, one intention drives everything at once: the blacksmith gets angry, so his brows drop, his voice tightens, his fist closes and his weight shifts forward, all in the same beat. This decision is where that unity comes from. It can come from one description of the moment that drives every part, from separate tracks lined up by hand, from an AI model that generates motion from the voice, or from you acting it out on camera. Each line gets one small, readable performance description: emotion, where the character looks, a gesture or two, a posture. Claude writes it from the script, and you review it like any change. The studio compiles it into separate channels (mouth from lip sync, face from emotion, hands from gestures and grips, body posture, eyes) that all share the same beats, so they move together. A procedural "life" layer adds blinks, small eye movements, breathing and weight shifts so nobody freezes. "Make him angrier" changes one emotion value and every channel follows.

Works well when

  • Lots of dialogue, directed in plain words
  • You want edits like "angrier" to touch everything
  • Every change stays reviewable

Watch out for

  • Gestures come from a library, not invented motion
  • The compiler's rules need careful tuning

Each part of the body gets its own track: lip sync on one, expressions on another, gestures, posture and eyes on their own. This is how traditional animation timelines work, and it gives full control. But unity is your job: change a line's timing and five tracks need re-aligning, and anything not deliberately animated (eyes, weight) sits frozen. "Make him angrier" means editing three tracks consistently. It's the opposite of the tied-together feel you asked for.

Works well when

  • A few hero shots polished by hand
  • An animator wants every curve

Watch out for

  • Coherence is manual and breaks on edits
  • Unanimated parts look frozen

Research models (EMAGE, TalkSHOW and others) generate face, body and hand motion together from the voice. The rhythm looks human, and coherence comes for free. But in the largest evaluation (GENEA 2023), generated gestures matched what was being said only slightly better than chance: natural movement that means nothing. Most also output a research skeleton (SMPL-X) whose licence forbids commercial games. There's nothing to review except "regenerate".

Works well when

  • Research or non-commercial projects

Watch out for

  • Gestures don't match meaning; can't direct them

The case against my pick

A performance script makes coherence a system of rules, and rule-driven characters can feel rule-driven. The same lean-in and the same palm-down gesture on every firm line, a little too tidy. Gestures come from a library Claude picks from, so nothing is ever invented in the moment, and the life layer's blinks and glances are statistics, not intention. For the scene that matters most emotionally, D's real performance will beat it. My answer: A for all dialogue, with D's capture available as an override for the few lines you want to act yourself, which compiles into the same channels.

This recommendation assumes

  • Cutscenes are dialogue-heavy and directed in words ("he's firm, then gentle"), which is what Claude does well.
  • A gesture library (palm-down, point, shrug, fist, open palms, counting…) built on decision 22's hand poses plus the body kit covers most acting.
  • The life layer (blinks, eye saccades, breathing, weight shifts) is procedural and seeded, so it's reproducible and reviewable.
  • The performance file is the studio's: plain text, diffable, a reviewable change like any other.

What the research says

  • Valve's FacePoser choreography files are the closest precedent. One plain-text scene file per scene holds lip sync from the audio and transcript, weighted expression layers, gesture events locked to timing tags, and positioning. Valve Developer Wiki Choreography reference
  • Night in the Woods drove animation from inside the dialogue script: stage-direction commands (sit, look at, pupil dilation) and emoticons in lines triggered expressions. "Just about all of the gameplay logic" ran through it. Yarn Spinner docs
  • In GENEA 2023's large crowdsourced evaluation, some generated gesture systems looked as human-like as motion capture. But their fit to what was being said sat just above chance, far behind real motion capture. GENEA 2023
  • The SMPL-X body model, the output of most gesture models, forbids commercial products. BEAT2 data is CC BY-NC, and TalkSHOW is research-only. SMPL-X licence TalkSHOW
  • "Eyes Alive" statistical eye movements, fitted to eye-tracker data with separate talking and listening modes, were rated better than still or random eyes. This is the basis for a procedural life layer. Lee, Badler & Badler, SIGGRAPH 2002
  • MetaHuman's audio-driven animation infers emotion from the voice and adds plausible head movement. It's a reference design for what one performance can drive, though it only works inside Unreal. Epic docs

Where experts disagree

Machine-learning researchers say generated whole-body motion is close to solving natural performance. The evaluations agree it looks natural, but show it doesn't yet carry meaning. Game dialogue tools from Valve to Night in the Woods chose authored, script-driven direction instead. A directed approach now, with ML as a plug-in source later (when a commercially licensed model fits your rig), gets both.
