How the Face Is Rigged

Architecture 🚪 One-way door High stakes

You chose expressive, stylized faces built into the kit (decision 19). Now: what moves them? There are two building blocks. Shape keys (also called morph targets or blend shapes) are pre-made versions of the face, such as "mouth O" or "brows up", that blend in by a percentage. Face bones are small joints that pull the face around. Both glTF engines play shape keys natively. The choice decides how expressions are named, what lip sync can drive, how big files get, and whether "smile 60%" is a setting Claude can review.

Generate all 52 ARKit shapes, the standard that iPhone face capture, Azure's text-to-speech and Audio2Face output directly. It's maximally compatible: plug any of them in and the face moves. But many of the 52 are subtle (mouthRollLower, mouthShrugUpper) and barely read on a stylized face, eyes and jaw as shapes look worse than bones, and morph-heavy models roughly double in file size. Known pitfall: jawOpen and mouthClose fight each other, so mouths don't close.

Drive the face entirely with small bones, like the body. Files stay small, everything is one skeleton, and it's the most common approach in mobile games. But bone-pulled faces on low-polygon meshes tend to look soft and mushy: a crisp "O" or a clean smile is much easier as a designed shape. Speech shapes become bone poses that every lip-sync source needs mapping into, so there are no standard names to plug into.

Works well when

  • Mobile, tight memory
  • Faces with plenty of polygons to pull

Watch out for

  • Soft, imprecise shapes on stylized faces
  • Every lip-sync source needs a custom mapping

The case against my pick

A custom subset is a bet that you won't want face capture later. An iPhone performance or Azure's text-to-speech output arrives as the full ARKit 52, and with A, about 36 of those channels have nowhere to go and must be folded into the ones you kept. That's a mapping table that loses subtlety. Shape keys also cost file size and have broken on some Android devices in both engines. If you expect to act out performances on your phone, B's full set is the safer long-term bet, at the cost of shapes that barely show on a stylized face.

This recommendation assumes

  • Lip sync comes mainly from audio or text (decision 21), which outputs the 15 standard speech shapes, not full ARKit capture.
  • The kit generates every shape from its face template, so new characters cost nothing extra.
  • A face-shape budget check joins the game profiles (decision 17): shape count, sparse storage, no shape normals on web.
  • If ARKit-52 input appears (phone capture, Azure), a fixed mapping table folds it into this set; it's lossy but works.

What the research says

  • Meta's 15 speech shapes (sil, PP, FF, TH, DD, kk, CH, SS, nn, RR, aa, E, ih, oh, ou) are the de facto naming standard. The SDK is end-of-life, but the names live on in open three.js avatar tools. Meta TalkingHead
  • Both engines play glTF shape keys natively: Godot as blend shapes with a dedicated animation track, three.js as morph target influences. Godot docs three.js forum
  • Azure's text-to-speech outputs 55 values per frame, the first 52 matching ARKit names, and Audio2Face can output ARKit-style weights. That's the case for B. Microsoft Learn Audio2Face paper
  • ARKit-52 rigs commonly fail with jawOpen fighting mouthClose so mouths don't shut, and with eye bones that aren't converted. Unreal forum
  • Morph-heavy models roughly doubled GLB size for one developer, and Draco doesn't compress morph targets. The fixes are sparse storage, no morph normals and quantization. PlayCanvas forum
  • Skinned meshes with shape keys failed on some Android GPUs in both Godot and three.js, so real-device testing is needed for mobile. Godot issue three.js report
